\begin{abstract}
DBLP-QA is a recent benchmark of 50 questions written from computer-science abstracts, introduced with
RAGScholar, a system that answers them with BM25 retrieval and small language models, rated by hand.
We re-evaluate it end to end with a fully automatic and reproducible protocol: an LLM judge applying
the benchmark's own 0--2 rubric, checked by controls on every run, with bootstrap confidence intervals
and paired comparisons. Four findings stand out. First, current models answer much of the benchmark
from memory: GPT-4.1 scores 1.46/2 with no retrieval, and an automatic audit classifies 58\% of the
questions as general knowledge. Given the source abstract, every model we test, including a 7B one,
scores at least 1.98, so the benchmark now measures retrieval. Second, the original Lucene index cannot
be rebuilt, so we reconstruct retrieval in the open world from four public search services; the source
paper reaches the top five for 58\% of questions, BM25 re-ranking comes within two points of the pool's
ceiling, and the hybrid retrieval proposed as future work does not help. Third, the gain from retrieval
tracks how often the source is retrieved: about zero for strong models at 36\%, $+0.30$ at 58\%. Fourth,
the apparent harm of retrieving the wrong papers is mostly an artefact of grading against one reference
answer: answers are rarely misled, and a symmetric multi-reference re-grade finds no detectable harm.
A relevance gate does not improve accuracy but halves a CPU-bound 7B model's inference time. All code,
prompts and run artefacts are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) conditions a language model's answer on documents retrieved for
the question~\cite{lewis2020rag,gao2023ragsurvey}. For scholarly information access it promises answers
that can be traced to publications rather than to a model's memory. Neekhra, Nilles and Schenkel
recently introduced DBLP-QA, a benchmark of 50 question--answer pairs written from abstracts of papers
indexed by dblp~\cite{ley2009dblp}, together with RAGScholar, a system that retrieves abstracts with
Lucene BM25 and answers with instruction-tuned models from 1.1B to 14.7B
parameters~\cite{neekhra2026ragscholar}. Answers were rated by hand on a 0--2 scale; the best
configuration, Mistral-7B with the top five abstracts concatenated, scored 1.74, against 0.80 without
retrieval.

Several questions remain open. The models evaluated are small, so it is unclear how much of the
benchmark a current model answers without retrieval at all. Retrieval was evaluated in a closed world:
the questions were sampled from the very collection that was indexed, and the abstract set behind that
index is no longer fully available, so the setting cannot be rebuilt. Each question has a single
reference answer taken from one abstract, which may penalise correct answers drawn from other papers.
And the ratings, made by hand, cannot be re-run when the system changes.

We address these questions with an evaluation that needs no manual rating and can be re-run at will.
Our research questions are:

\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] How much of \dq{} do current models answer without retrieval, and what do they score when
  given the source abstract?
\item[RQ2] How often can the source paper be retrieved in an open-world setting today, and do dense or
  hybrid rankers help?
\item[RQ3] How does the benefit of retrieval depend on retrieving the source, and do the wrong papers'
  abstracts hurt?
\item[RQ4] Can selective retrieval keep the gains without the losses?
\item[RQ5] How robust are the conclusions to the grading protocol?
\end{description}

Our contributions are: (i)~an automatic evaluation harness for \dq{}, with an LLM judge on the
benchmark's own rubric gated by controls, bootstrap intervals, paired comparisons and per-question
audits; (ii)~an open-world reconstruction of retrieval from public services, with a frozen candidate
pool so that compared runs see identical candidates; (iii)~evidence that retrieval success, not
generation, now decides performance; (iv)~a demonstration that the benchmark's single-reference grading
turns valid alternative answers into apparent retrieval harm; and (v)~an evaluation of two selective
retrieval strategies. The harness is part of an open dblp exploration system and runs on a single CPU
machine; all runs together cost under US\$5 in API fees.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Scholarly QA over dblp.} DBLP-QuAD~\cite{banerjee2023dblpquad} asks questions over the dblp
knowledge graph (authors, venues, years); DBLP-QA~\cite{neekhra2026ragscholar} instead asks about the
content of papers, answerable from their abstracts, and is the benchmark we re-evaluate.

\textbf{Retrieval, distractors and selective retrieval.} Irrelevant context can mislead language
models~\cite{shi2023distracted}, the position of the relevant passage matters~\cite{liu2024lost}, and
the effect of non-relevant documents in RAG is not uniformly negative~\cite{cuconasu2024noise}. Whether
to retrieve at all depends on how well a model already knows the answer~\cite{mallen2023trust}, which
motivates adaptive and self-reflective schemes such as Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag},
CRAG~\cite{yan2024crag} and Adaptive-RAG~\cite{jeong2024adaptiverag}. Our gated condition is a simple
member of this family: a separate model judges the relevance of each retrieved abstract.

\textbf{Evaluation.} LLM judges agree well with humans on many tasks but have biases, including a
preference for their own generations~\cite{zheng2023judge,panickssery2024selfpref}. Lexical and
single-reference evaluation underestimates open-domain QA systems whose correct answers are phrased
differently or differ in content~\cite{kamalloo2023evaluating}; we find the same effect in a RAG setting.
Benchmarks built from public text may also be contaminated by pre-training data~\cite{sainz2023contamination}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Benchmark and conditions}

\dq{} has 50 questions, each with a 1--3 sentence reference answer, the dblp key of its source paper and
its Semantic Scholar corpus id. We fetch source abstracts from Semantic Scholar~\cite{kinney2023s2},
falling back to arXiv, OpenAlex~\cite{priem2022openalex} and Crossref; 46 of the 50 abstracts are
available (21 from Semantic Scholar, 25 from OpenAlex). We compare five conditions:

\begin{description}
\item[Closed-book] the question alone.
\item[Oracle] the question with its source abstract (46 questions).
\item[RAG] the question with the top five retrieved abstracts, titled and concatenated: the best
  strategy of the original study (Top-5-CD).
\item[RAG, permissive] as RAG, but the prompt says the abstracts may not be relevant and to answer from
  the model's own knowledge if none addresses the question.
\item[RAG, gated] a separate call to gpt-4.1-mini keeps only the retrieved abstracts that address the
  question; with none kept, the question is asked closed-book. Verdicts are cached, so every answering
  model receives the same filtered context.
\end{description}

All prompts ask for an answer of one to three sentences (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).

\subsection{Models}

We answer with gpt-4.1-mini and gpt-4.1 at temperature~0, and with Mistral-7B-Instruct
v0.1~\cite{jiang2023mistral}, the best model of the original study, using its published settings
(temperature 0.7, top-$p$ 0.9, at most 512 tokens), served 4-bit quantised by Ollama on six CPU cores.

\subsection{Automatic grading with controls}
\label{sec:judge}

A gpt-4.1 judge grades every answer against the reference on the benchmark's scale (2 correct and
complete, 1 correct but incomplete, 0 incorrect or irrelevant), anchored with the original paper's own
worked example. A judge is used only if it passes two controls on the 50 questions, re-checked whenever
the judge or dataset changes: each reference answer must score 2, and another question's reference
answer (a derangement) must score 0, both in at least 95\% of cases. gpt-4.1 passed both 50/50. Means
are reported with 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals (10{,}000 resamples, fixed seed)~\cite{efron1993bootstrap};
comparisons between conditions are paired by question, with the mean difference, its bootstrap interval
and the numbers of questions that improved and worsened. ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge} is recorded but not
used for conclusions.

\textbf{No direct comparison with the original scores.} To calibrate, we re-ran the original study's
closed-book condition with its own models and settings. Our judge gave Mistral-7B 1.04 (the original
reports 0.80) and TinyLlama-1.1B 0.58 (original: 1.10), reversing their order. Possible causes include
rater differences, quantisation and single samples at temperature~0.7. We therefore never compare our
numbers with the original paper's; every comparison below is between conditions graded by the same
judge.\footnote{For reproducibility we also note that the original reports BM25 with $b=2$, outside
the usual $[0,1]$ range; that its source-rank counts (32, 7, 2, 1 and 5 outside the top five) sum to 47
of 50 questions; and that several of its mean ratings (e.g.\ 1.63, 1.49, 0.014) are not attainable as
means of 50 integer ratings.}

\subsection{Open-world retrieval}
\label{sec:retrieval}

The original index held 4.6 million Semantic Scholar abstracts from April~2025, a set its authors note
is no longer fully available~\cite{neekhra2026ragscholar}. We instead build, for each question, a
candidate pool from four first-stage retrievers, keeping only papers in dblp: (a)~dblp-search, our
hybrid title search over the full dblp dump (top 50); (b)~Semantic Scholar relevance search (top 100);
(c)~OpenAlex keyword search over titles, abstracts and full text, with the question's content words
OR-ed (OpenAlex otherwise requires every word to match), top 50; and (d)~OpenAlex semantic search, which
embeds titles and abstracts of all works with GTE-large~\cite{li2023gte}, top 50. OpenAlex works are
mapped to dblp records by DOI, arXiv identifier or exact normalised title; 1{,}151 of the 4{,}830 works
returned are in dblp. Abstracts for the pool come with the OpenAlex results or are fetched by DOI. The
pool averages 73.7 candidates per question, 76\% of them with an abstract.

Three rankers order the same pool: BM25 (Lucene's formula, $k_1=1.2$, $b=0.75$, pool-level
idf)~\cite{robertson2009bm25} over title and abstract; dense cosine similarity of text-embedding-3-large
vectors (512 dimensions); and reciprocal rank fusion~\cite{cormack2009rrf} of the two ($k=60$), the
hybrid proposed as future work in the original study. A candidate counts as the source paper if it has
the benchmark's dblp key, Semantic Scholar's dblp key for the benchmark's paper, or the same normalised
title (a preprint and its published version are different dblp records). When the source is retrieved,
it is shown with the same abstract as in the oracle condition.

The pool is built once and frozen; every RAG run records its fingerprint, and runs are compared only on
the same pool. Semantic Scholar's unauthenticated search was saturated during construction and refused
one question's search through all retries; that question's pool lacks those candidates.

\subsection{Implementation}

The harness is a Python module of the dblp Explorer system, run as a batch container next to its search
and analysis services on one CPU virtual machine. Each experiment is one command
(\texttt{dblpqa closed-book|oracle|retrieval|rag|audit|regrade|crossjudge}); answers are written as they
are produced, every external response (abstracts, searches, embeddings, judge and gate verdicts) is
cached, and a run interrupted by an exhausted API credit can be completed later without repeating paid
calls. The test suite runs every component against simulated services.

Without retrieval, gpt-4.1-mini and gpt-4.1 score 1.40 and 1.46 (Table~\ref{tab:main}); only five of
50 answers from each are graded incorrect. Given the source abstract, all three models score 1.98 or
more, Mistral-7B included, and no answer is worse than its closed-book counterpart. Generation is
therefore solved on this benchmark: what varies between systems is whether they retrieve the right
abstract, and how much the model already knows.

An automatic audit explains the second part. Two labellers, gpt-4.1 and gpt-4.1-mini, independently
classify every question, seeing the question, reference and source abstract, as \emph{general}
(standard knowledge an expert would give without the paper), \emph{identifiable} (paper-specific and
detailed enough to locate the paper) or \emph{underspecified} (paper-specific but answerable by many

(Table~\ref{tab:audit}). Closed-book, the GPT models score about 1.8 on general questions and about 1.0
on identifiable ones; retrieval adds 0.14 on the former and about 0.5 on the latter.

The source paper is in the pool for 60\% of questions (Table~\ref{tab:retrieval}). BM25 over title and
abstract ranks it in the top five for 58\%, two points short of that ceiling, and first for 42\%. Dense
retrieval is weaker, and fusing it with BM25 does not improve on BM25 alone: the questions were written
from their abstracts and share their wording, which favours lexical matching once the source is a
candidate. No single public service comes close; OpenAlex's semantic search finds the source within its
top 50 for 44\% of questions. For 40\% of questions none of the four services returns the source at
all. An earlier pool without the OpenAlex retrievers held the source for at most 38\% of questions, and
BM25 placed it in the top five for 36--38\%.

These numbers are not comparable with the original study's, whose BM25 placed the source first for 32 of
50 questions: there, every question was written from a paper inside the indexed collection, a closed
world that guarantees the source is a candidate. Our open-world figure is what a system built today from
public services achieves.

Retrieval helps every model significantly on the frozen pool: $+0.30$ to $+0.32$ for the GPT models
and $+0.56$ for Mistral-7B, with intervals well above zero (Table~\ref{tab:main}). On the earlier pool,
where the source reached the context for 36\% of questions, the same method gained only $+0.08$ for
both GPT models (intervals $-0.12$ to $+0.30$ and $-0.12$ to $+0.28$) and $+0.38$ for Mistral-7B. Figure~\ref{fig:recall} shows the
pattern: the score rises with the share of questions whose source is retrieved, towards the oracle's
ceiling.

The split by retrieval outcome explains why. On the 29 questions whose source was among the top five,
the GPT models score 1.97 with plain RAG ($+0.55$ and $+0.66$ over closed-book) and Mistral-7B 1.83;
across the nine RAG runs on this pool, only one answer is worse than its closed-book counterpart. On the
21 questions whose source was missed, graded against the reference, the GPT models fall slightly below
their closed-book scores (gpt-4.1-mini $-0.14$, gpt-4.1 $-0.05$; Table~\ref{tab:missed}), while Mistral-7B
gains ($+0.14$). On the earlier pool this apparent harm was larger ($-0.28$ and $-0.19$ for the GPT
models on 32 missed questions).

\textbf{Is the harm real?} We audit every answer that lost points on a missed question (98 answers over
the nine runs; same two labellers, $\kappa=0.78$, 89 of 98 agreeing). gpt-4.1 labels 75 of them
\emph{valid other paper}, a correct answer to the question as worded, supported by a retrieved
abstract from a different paper; 6 \emph{misled}, all from Mistral-7B; and 17 \emph{own knowledge}.
None of the 57 GPT answers that lost points was misled. Because only RAG answers were audited, and
the labellers had no controls, we then re-grade all twelve answer sets (three models $\times$
closed-book and three RAG modes) on the 21 missed questions with a symmetric multi-reference judge: the
same gpt-4.1, shown the reference and the same five retrieved abstracts for every answer, credits any
answer that is correct for the question as worded, whether supported by an abstract or established
knowledge. It must first pass three controls on these questions: the reference scores 2 (21/21),
another question's reference scores 0 (20/21), and another question's RAG answer scores 0 (21/21); the
last control catches a judge that rewards any fluent, on-topic text.

Re-grading credits closed-book answers too (gpt-4.1: $1.52\rightarrow1.81$), so the audit alone would
have overstated retrieval. Under the symmetric grade, no condition differs detectably from closed-book
on the missed questions: every difference for the GPT models lies within $\pm0.10$ with an interval
spanning zero, and Mistral-7B's differences are positive ($+0.10$ to $+0.24$) with intervals that touch
zero (Table~\ref{tab:missed}). The harm measured against the single reference was therefore mostly a
grading artefact: abstracts from the wrong papers led the models to different correct answers, not to
wrong ones.

\subsection{RQ4: selective retrieval}

Neither selective strategy reliably beats plain RAG. The permissive prompt leaves gpt-4.1-mini and
Mistral-7B unchanged and raises gpt-4.1 to 1.84, $+0.08$ over plain RAG ($+0.02$ to $+0.16$), from four
questions that improved and none that worsened; under the fair re-grade it is the only condition in
which gpt-4.1 loses on none of the missed questions. The gate keeps the source abstract on 27 of the 29 questions where it was
retrieved, but discards every abstract on only 2 of the 21 where it was missed: it judges the wrong
papers' abstracts relevant, because they address the topic of the question. It keeps 1.74 abstracts on
average, and its accuracy is within $0.06$ of plain RAG for every model. Its benefit is cost: Mistral-7B,
reading 1.74 abstracts instead of five, answered the 50 questions in 30 minutes instead of 60 on the same
CPU, at an identical mean score. Gating all 50 questions cost US\$0.02.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{What \dq{} measures.} With current models, \dq{} measures two things: whether the source
abstract is retrieved, and how much the model knows already. Generation given the right abstract is
saturated, and more than half the questions are general knowledge. A system's score therefore depends
on its retriever and its model's memory more than on how it uses context, the aspect the original
context strategies were designed to compare.

\textbf{Recommendations for scholarly RAG benchmarks.} Report closed-book and oracle conditions next to
retrieval, so that memory and the generation ceiling are visible. Evaluate retrieval in an open world, by
the rate at which the source reaches the context, since a closed-world index guarantees the source is a
candidate. Grade against multiple references, or with a judge that may credit other correct answers
under controls, because single-reference grading turns valid alternatives into apparent errors. And
prefer questions that identify their paper, or questions written from papers published after the
evaluated models' training data, so that retrieval is necessary.

\textbf{Limitations.} Fifty questions give wide intervals, and subgroup analyses (29 and 21 questions)
wider still. Mistral-7B was sampled once per question at temperature~0.7, and quantised. Every grade
comes from an LLM judge; controls, inter-labeller agreement, the symmetric re-grade and the
cross-family judge bound but do not remove that dependence, and no human ratings were collected. The
training data of the GPT models is unknown, so we cannot say which questions they saw. Our open-world
pool depends on public services, one of which refused a search; BM25 uses pool-level statistics. The
audit labels are themselves model judgements.

\textbf{Future work.} A fresh companion set, generated automatically from papers published after the
models' training cutoff and filtered to be unanswerable without its abstract, answerable with it and
specific to its paper, would test the memorisation finding directly.

%% ===================================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Re-evaluated with current models and an automatic, controlled protocol, \dq{} no longer distinguishes
how systems use retrieved context: given the source abstract every model is near perfect, and without
any context strong models already score about 1.4--1.5 of 2. What separates systems is whether
retrieval finds the source, which in an open world succeeds for 58\% of questions at best; the gain from
retrieval follows that rate. The harm that retrieving the wrong papers appears to cause disappears when
correct alternative answers are credited. For scholarly RAG evaluation, we recommend reporting
closed-book and oracle bounds, open-world source recall, and multi-reference grading.

\section{Prompts}
\label{app:prompts}

\textbf{Closed-book.} \emph{System:} ``You answer questions about computer-science research. Answer in
one to three sentences.'' \emph{User:} the question.

\textbf{Oracle.} \emph{System:} ``You answer questions about computer-science research using the
abstract you are given. Answer in one to three sentences.'' \emph{User:} ``Abstract: \ldots{} Question:
\ldots''

\textbf{RAG and gated.} As the oracle, with ``the paper abstracts you are given'' and the numbered,
titled abstracts.

\textbf{RAG permissive.} ``You answer questions about computer-science research. You are given abstracts
of papers a search returned for the question; they may or may not be relevant. Use them where they
address the question; if none does, ignore them and answer from your own knowledge. Answer in one to
three sentences.''

\textbf{Gate.} ``You check search results for a question about computer-science research. You are given
the question and numbered paper abstracts. Say which abstracts contain information that answers the
question; an abstract on the same broad topic that does not address what is asked does not count.''
It replies with the numbers of the abstracts to keep.

\textbf{Judge.} The benchmark's 0--2 scale with the original paper's worked example (its Table~2); it
grades only against the reference, does not penalise extra correct detail, and does not reward fluency.

\textbf{Multi-reference judge.} The same scale, with the reference and the five retrieved abstracts:
2 if the answer matches the reference \emph{or} is an equally valid answer to the question as worded,
supported by one of the abstracts or established knowledge.
