\title{Finding the Paper Is the Hard Part: A Re-evaluation of Retrieval-Augmented Scholarly QA on
DBLP-QA}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Hadi Ghazi}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Lebanese University}\\
hadigghazi@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
DBLP-QA is a benchmark of 50 questions written from the abstracts of computer-science papers. It was
introduced with RAGScholar, a system that retrieves abstracts with BM25 and answers with small language
models, and its answers were graded by hand. We re-evaluate it with current models and fully automatic
grading: a language-model judge applies the benchmark's 0--2 scale and must pass control checks before its
grades are used, and a second judge from a different model family checks the results. We also build
\fresh{}, 100 questions of the same kind about papers published after the models were trained. Answering
is no longer the problem: given the right abstract, every model scores at least 1.96 out of 2. Without any
retrieval, GPT-4.1 already scores 1.46, because most DBLP-QA questions ask about general knowledge; on
questions about a specific paper, the models know no more about older papers than about new ones. What
decides the score is whether retrieval finds the right paper. Using today's public search services, it is
among the top five results for 58\% of DBLP-QA questions and 89\% of Fresh questions, and the benefit of
retrieval grows with that rate. When retrieval misses, the wrong papers seem to cost points, but only
because answers are graded against a single reference answer: once any correct answer counts, neither
judge finds a cost.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
retrieval-augmented generation, scholarly question answering, dblp, LLM-as-a-judge, benchmark evaluation
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) conditions a language model's answer on documents retrieved for
the question~\cite{lewis2020rag,gao2023ragsurvey}. For scholarly information access it promises answers
that can be traced to publications rather than to a model's memory. Neekhra, Nilles and Schenkel recently
introduced DBLP-QA, a benchmark of 50 question--answer pairs written from abstracts of papers indexed by
dblp~\cite{ley2009dblp}, together with RAGScholar, a system that retrieves abstracts with Lucene BM25 and
answers with instruction-tuned models from 1.1B to 14.7B parameters~\cite{neekhra2026ragscholar}.
Answers were rated by hand on a 0--2 scale; the best configuration, Mistral-7B with the top five
abstracts concatenated, scored 1.74, against 0.80 without retrieval.

Several questions remain open. The models evaluated are small, so it is unclear how much of the
benchmark a current model answers without retrieval, and whether it does so from general knowledge or
from having read the papers. Retrieval was evaluated in a closed world: the questions were sampled from
the very collection that was indexed, and the abstract set behind that index is no longer fully
available, so the setting cannot be rebuilt. Each question has a single reference answer taken from one
abstract, which may penalise correct answers drawn from other papers. And the ratings, made by hand,
cannot be re-run when the system changes.

We address these questions with an evaluation that needs no manual rating and can be re-run at will.
Our research questions are:

\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] How much do current models answer without retrieval, what do they score given the source
  abstract, and does their prior knowledge come from memory of the papers?
\item[RQ2] How often can the source paper be retrieved in an open-world setting today, and do dense or
  hybrid rankers help?
\item[RQ3] How does the benefit of retrieval depend on retrieving the source, and do the wrong papers'
  abstracts hurt?
\item[RQ4] Can selective retrieval keep the gains without the losses?
\item[RQ5] How robust are the conclusions to the grading protocol and the judge?
\end{description}

Our contributions are: (i)~an automatic evaluation harness for \dq{}, with an LLM judge on the
benchmark's own rubric gated by controls, a second judge from another model family, bootstrap intervals,
paired comparisons and per-question audits; (ii)~\fresh{}, an automatically built companion set of 100
questions about 2025--2026 papers; (iii)~an open-world reconstruction of retrieval from public services,
with a frozen candidate pool so that compared runs see identical candidates; (iv)~evidence that
retrieval success, not generation, now decides performance, and that the models' prior knowledge is
knowledge of the field rather than memory of the papers; (v)~a demonstration that single-reference
grading decides how much retrieving the wrong papers appears to cost; and (vi)~an evaluation of two
selective retrieval strategies. The harness is part of an open dblp exploration system and runs on a
single CPU machine; all runs together cost under US\$8 in API fees.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Scholarly QA over dblp.} DBLP-QuAD~\cite{banerjee2023dblpquad} asks questions over the dblp
knowledge graph (authors, venues, years); DBLP-QA~\cite{neekhra2026ragscholar} instead asks about the
content of papers, answerable from their abstracts, and is the benchmark we re-evaluate.

\textbf{Retrieval, distractors and selective retrieval.} Irrelevant context can mislead language
models~\cite{shi2023distracted}, the position of the relevant passage matters~\cite{liu2024lost}, and
the effect of non-relevant documents in RAG is not uniformly negative~\cite{cuconasu2024noise}. Whether
to retrieve at all depends on how well a model already knows the answer~\cite{mallen2023trust}, which
motivates adaptive and self-reflective schemes such as Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag},
CRAG~\cite{yan2024crag} and Adaptive-RAG~\cite{jeong2024adaptiverag}. Our gated condition is a simple
member of this family: a separate model judges the relevance of each retrieved abstract.

\textbf{Evaluation.} LLM judges agree well with humans on many tasks but have biases, including a
preference for their own generations~\cite{zheng2023judge,panickssery2024selfpref}. Lexical and
single-reference evaluation underestimates open-domain QA systems whose correct answers are phrased
differently or differ in content~\cite{kamalloo2023evaluating}; we find the same effect in a RAG
setting. Benchmarks built from public text may also be contaminated by pre-training
data~\cite{sainz2023contamination}; a set built from papers newer than the models avoids this by
construction.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Benchmark and Conditions}

\dq{} has 50 questions, each with a 1--3 sentence reference answer, the dblp key of its source paper and
its Semantic Scholar corpus id. We fetch source abstracts from Semantic Scholar~\cite{kinney2023s2},
falling back to arXiv, OpenAlex~\cite{priem2022openalex} and Crossref; 46 of the 50 abstracts are
available (21 from Semantic Scholar, 25 from OpenAlex). We compare five conditions:

%% itemize, not description: IEEEtran's description labels have a fixed width and overlap long ones
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Closed-book:} the question alone.
\item \textbf{Oracle:} the question with its source abstract (46 questions).
\item \textbf{RAG:} the question with the top five retrieved abstracts, titled and concatenated: the
  best strategy of the original study (Top-5-CD).
\item \textbf{RAG, permissive:} as RAG, but the prompt says the abstracts may not be relevant and to
  answer from the model's own knowledge if none addresses the question.
\item \textbf{RAG, gated:} a separate call to gpt-4.1-mini keeps only the retrieved abstracts that
  address the question; with none kept, the question is asked closed-book. Verdicts are cached, so every
  answering model receives the same filtered context.
\end{itemize}

All prompts ask for an answer of one to three sentences (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).

\subsection{\fresh{}}
\label{sec:fresh}

To separate knowledge of a field from memory of particular papers, we build a companion set the way
\dq{}'s authors built theirs, but automatically. We sample, with a fixed seed, journal and conference
papers in dblp published in 2025 or later, after GPT-4.1's June 2024 training cutoff and Mistral-7B
v0.1's 2023 release, excluding preprints, and fetch their abstracts by DOI. gpt-4.1 writes one question
per abstract with a 1--3 sentence answer taken from it, using four \dq{} pairs as style examples and
required to stand on its own (no ``this paper''). A question is kept only if gpt-4.1-mini, given the
abstract, answers it fully (judge score 2). Of 145 papers read, 100 questions were kept; 38 papers had no
abstract, 5 a short abstract or an unusable question, and for 2 the abstract did not answer the
generated question. The build cost US\$0.36. No question is filtered on closed-book answers, which would
make the models fail by construction; instead \fresh{} goes through the same audit as \dq{}, and the two
are compared within audit labels.

\subsection{Models}

We answer with gpt-4.1-mini and gpt-4.1 at temperature~0, and with Mistral-7B-Instruct
v0.1~\cite{jiang2023mistral}, the best model of the original study, using its published settings
(temperature 0.7, top-$p$ 0.9, at most 512 tokens), served 4-bit quantised by Ollama on six CPU cores.

\subsection{Automatic Grading with Controls}
\label{sec:judge}

A gpt-4.1 judge grades every answer against the reference on the benchmark's scale (2 correct and
complete, 1 correct but incomplete, 0 incorrect or irrelevant), anchored with the original paper's own
worked example. A judge is used only if it passes two controls on the whole question set, re-checked
whenever the judge or dataset changes: each reference answer must score 2, and another question's
reference answer (a derangement) must score 0, both in at least 95\% of cases. gpt-4.1 passed both 50/50
on \dq{} and 100/100 and 98/100 on \fresh{}. Means are reported with 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals
(10{,}000 resamples, fixed seed)~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}; comparisons between conditions are paired by
question, with the mean difference, its bootstrap interval and the numbers of questions that improved and
worsened. A second judge from another family, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct~\cite{qwen2024qwen25}, re-grades the
main answer sets (Section~\ref{sec:rq5}). ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge} is recorded but not used for
conclusions.

\textbf{No direct comparison with the original scores.} To calibrate, we re-ran the original study's
closed-book condition with its own models and settings. Our judge gave Mistral-7B 1.04 (the original
reports 0.80) and TinyLlama-1.1B 0.58 (original: 1.10), reversing their order. Possible causes include
rater differences, quantisation and single samples at temperature~0.7. We therefore never compare our
numbers with the original paper's; every comparison below is between conditions graded by the same
judge.\footnote{For reproducibility we also note that the original reports BM25 with $b=2$, outside the
usual $[0,1]$ range; that its source-rank counts (32, 7, 2, 1 and 5 outside the top five) sum to 47 of
50 questions; and that several of its mean ratings (e.g.\ 1.63, 1.49, 0.014) are not attainable as means
of 50 integer ratings.}

\subsection{Open-World Retrieval}
\label{sec:retrieval}

The original index held 4.6 million Semantic Scholar abstracts from April~2025, a set its authors note
is no longer fully available~\cite{neekhra2026ragscholar}. We instead build, for each question, a
candidate pool from four first-stage retrievers, keeping only papers in dblp: (a)~dblp-search, our
hybrid title search over the full dblp dump (top 50); (b)~Semantic Scholar relevance search (top 100);
(c)~OpenAlex keyword search over titles, abstracts and full text, with the question's content words
OR-ed (OpenAlex otherwise requires every word to match), top 50; and (d)~OpenAlex semantic search,
which embeds titles and abstracts of all works with GTE-large~\cite{li2023gte}, top 50. OpenAlex works
are mapped to dblp records by DOI, arXiv identifier or exact normalised title (1{,}151 of 4{,}830 works
for \dq{}, 2{,}868 of 9{,}409 for \fresh{}). Abstracts for the pool come with the OpenAlex results or are
fetched by DOI. Pools average 73 candidates per question, 76\% of them with an abstract.

Three rankers order the same pool: BM25 (Lucene's formula, $k_1=1.2$, $b=0.75$, pool-level
idf)~\cite{robertson2009bm25} over title and abstract; dense cosine similarity of
text-embedding-3-large vectors (512 dimensions); and reciprocal rank fusion~\cite{cormack2009rrf} of the
two ($k=60$), the hybrid proposed as future work in the original study. A candidate counts as the source
paper if it has the benchmark's dblp key, Semantic Scholar's dblp key for the benchmark's paper, or the
same normalised title (a preprint and its published version are different dblp records). When the
source is retrieved, it is shown with the same abstract as in the oracle condition.

Each pool is built once and frozen; every RAG run records its fingerprint, and runs are compared only on
the same pool. Semantic Scholar's unauthenticated search was saturated: it refused one \dq{} question's
search through all retries and 96 of 100 for \fresh{}, so those pools lack its candidates, which on \dq{}
contained the source for only 8\% of questions.

\subsection{Implementation}

The harness is a Python module of the dblp Explorer system, run as a batch container next to its
search and analysis services on one CPU virtual machine. Each experiment is one command; answers are
written as they are produced, every external response (abstracts, searches, embeddings, judge and gate
verdicts) is cached, and a run interrupted by an exhausted API credit can be completed later without
repeating paid calls. The test suite runs every component against simulated services.

\subsection{RQ1: Prior Knowledge and the Generation Ceiling}

Without retrieval, gpt-4.1-mini and gpt-4.1 score 1.40 and 1.46 on \dq{} (Table~\ref{tab:main}); only
five of 50 answers from each are graded incorrect. Given the source abstract, all three models score
1.98 or more, and no answer is worse than its closed-book counterpart. Generation is therefore solved on
this benchmark: what varies between systems is whether they retrieve the right abstract, and how much
the model already knows.

An automatic audit shows where that knowledge lies. Two labellers, gpt-4.1 and gpt-4.1-mini,
independently classify every question, seeing the question, reference and source abstract, as
\emph{general} (standard knowledge an expert would give without the paper), \emph{identifiable}
(paper-specific and detailed enough to locate the paper) or \emph{underspecified} (paper-specific but
answerable by many papers in different ways). On \dq{} they agree on 42 of 50 questions (Cohen's
$\kappa=0.70$~\cite{cohen1960kappa}). By gpt-4.1's labels, 29 questions (58\%) are general, 20
identifiable and one underspecified (Table~\ref{tab:audit}). Closed-book, the GPT models score about 1.8
on general questions and about 1.0 on identifiable ones; Mistral-7B scores 1.34 and 0.65.

On \dq{} the source paper is in the pool for 60\% of questions (Table~\ref{tab:retrieval}). BM25 over
title and abstract ranks it in the top five for 58\%, two points short of that ceiling, and first for
42\%. Dense retrieval is weaker, and fusing it with BM25 does not improve on BM25 alone: the questions
were written from their abstracts and share their wording, which favours lexical matching once the
source is a candidate. No single public service comes close; OpenAlex's semantic search finds the source
within its top 50 for 44\% of questions, and for 40\% none of the four services returns it at all. An
earlier pool without the OpenAlex retrievers held the source for at most 38\% of questions, and BM25
placed it in the top five for 36--38\%.

\fresh{} is much easier to retrieve: the source is in the pool for 91\% of questions and BM25 places it
first for 82\% and in the top five for 89\%. Its questions are paper-specific and were written by a
model from the abstract, and recent papers are well covered by OpenAlex's semantic search (88\% found).
The ranking of methods is the same: BM25 equals the hybrid and beats dense retrieval.

These numbers are not comparable with the original study's, whose BM25 placed the source first for 32
of 50 questions: there, every question was written from a paper inside the indexed collection, a closed
world that guarantees the source is a candidate. Our open-world figures are what a system built today
from public services achieves.

$+0.32$ for the GPT models and $+0.56$ for Mistral-7B (Table~\ref{tab:main}). On the earlier pool, where
the source reached the context for 36\% of questions, the same method gained only $+0.08$ for both GPT
models (intervals $-0.12$ to $+0.30$ and $-0.12$ to $+0.28$) and $+0.38$ for Mistral-7B. On \fresh{},
where it reaches the context for 89\%, plain RAG raises both GPT models to 1.82 and Mistral-7B to 1.73,
gains of $+0.78$, $+0.86$ and $+0.95$ (Table~\ref{tab:fresh}). Because the two sets start from different
closed-book levels, Figure~\ref{fig:recall} expresses each gain as a share of the oracle's: at 58\%
retrieval RAG recovers 54--58\% of the oracle's gain, and at 89\% it recovers 80--84\%, for every model.

The split by retrieval outcome explains why. On the 29 \dq{} questions whose source was among the top
five, the GPT models score 1.97 with plain RAG ($+0.55$ and $+0.66$ over closed-book) and Mistral-7B 1.83;
across the nine RAG runs on this pool, only one answer is worse than its closed-book counterpart. On the
89 such Fresh questions the GPT models score 1.97 and 1.99 and Mistral-7B 1.86, and one answer in the
three runs is worse than closed-book. On the 21 \dq{} questions whose source was missed, graded against
the reference, the GPT models fall slightly below their closed-book scores (gpt-4.1-mini $-0.14$,
gpt-4.1 $-0.05$; Table~\ref{tab:missed}), while Mistral-7B gains ($+0.14$); on the earlier pool this
apparent harm was larger ($-0.28$ and $-0.19$ on 32 missed questions), and on the 11 missed Fresh
questions it is $-0.27$ and $-0.09$ for the GPT models and $+0.18$ for Mistral-7B.

\textbf{Is the harm real?} We audit every answer that lost points on a missed \dq{} question (98 answers
over the nine runs; same two labellers, $\kappa=0.78$, 89 of 98 agreeing). gpt-4.1 labels 75 of them
\emph{valid other paper}, a correct answer to the question as worded, supported by a retrieved abstract
from a different paper; 6 \emph{misled}, all from Mistral-7B; and 17 \emph{own knowledge}. These labels
are generous: on \fresh{} the same labeller called 30 of the 32 answers that lost points valid, although
those questions are paper-specific. We therefore rely on a controlled re-grade. All twelve answer sets (three
models $\times$ closed-book and three RAG modes) on the 21 missed \dq{} questions are graded again by a
symmetric multi-reference judge: the same gpt-4.1, shown the reference and the same five retrieved
abstracts for every answer, credits any answer that is correct for the question as worded, whether
supported by an abstract or established knowledge. It must first pass three controls on these
questions: the reference scores 2 (21/21), another question's reference scores 0 (20/21), and another
question's RAG answer scores 0 (21/21); the last control catches a judge that rewards any fluent,
on-topic text.

answers, not to wrong ones. Section~\ref{sec:rq5} examines whether a second judge agrees.

\subsection{RQ4: Selective Retrieval}

Neither selective strategy reliably beats plain RAG. The permissive prompt leaves gpt-4.1-mini and
Mistral-7B unchanged and raises gpt-4.1 to 1.84, $+0.08$ over plain RAG ($+0.02$ to $+0.16$), from four
questions that improved and none that worsened; under the fair re-grade it is the only condition in
which gpt-4.1 loses on none of the missed questions. The gate keeps the source abstract on 27 of the 29
questions where it was retrieved, but discards every abstract on only 2 of the 21 where it was missed:
it judges the wrong papers' abstracts relevant, because they address the topic of the question. It keeps
1.74 abstracts on average, and its accuracy is within $0.06$ of plain RAG for every model. Its benefit
is cost: Mistral-7B, reading 1.74 abstracts instead of five, answered the 50 questions in 30 minutes
instead of 60 on the same CPU, at an identical mean score. Gating all 50 questions cost US\$0.02.

\subsection{RQ5: Robustness to the Judge}
\label{sec:rq5}

gpt-4.1 grades answers from its own model family, and LLM judges can prefer their own
generations~\cite{panickssery2024selfpref}. We therefore re-judge the closed-book, plain RAG and oracle
answer sets of all three models on \dq{} with Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, an open model from another family
served on the same CPU machine, with the same rubric and after the same controls, which it passed 50/50
and 50/50.

The judges agree on 359 of 438 answers (82\%; Cohen's $\kappa=0.49$, quadratic-weighted
$\kappa=0.76$~\cite{cohen1968weighted}), and 70 of the 79 disagreements are between 1 and 2; only one
answer received 2 from one judge and 0 from the other. They differ in level: Qwen is more lenient with
closed-book answers (gpt-4.1: 1.46 by gpt-4.1, 1.66 by Qwen) and stricter with answers built on retrieved
or oracle abstracts (Mistral-7B with RAG: 1.60 and 1.32). There is no sign of self-preference: the gap
between the two judges is $-0.05$ for gpt-4.1's answers and $+0.02$ for gpt-4.1-mini's, but $+0.14$
($+0.06$ to $+0.21$) for Mistral-7B's, so gpt-4.1 is, if anything, more generous to the other family.

original order, with the answer before the abstracts.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{What \dq{} measures.} With current models, \dq{} measures three things: whether the source
abstract is retrieved; how much the model knows about the field, which on a set where 58\% of questions
are general knowledge is a great deal; and how the grader treats correct answers that are not the
reference answer. Generation given the right abstract is saturated on both sets, and the models'
knowledge is of the field, not of the papers: on paper-specific questions they know no more about \dq{}'s
papers than about papers published after their training data. A system's score therefore depends on its
retriever, its model's general knowledge and the grading protocol more than on how it uses context, the
aspect the original context strategies were designed to compare.

\textbf{Recommendations for scholarly RAG benchmarks.} Report closed-book and oracle conditions next to
retrieval, so that prior knowledge and the generation ceiling are visible. Evaluate retrieval in an open
world, by the rate at which the source reaches the context, since a closed-world index guarantees the
source is a candidate. Grade against multiple references, or with a judge that may credit other correct
answers under controls, and report results under at least two judges from different families, because
the cost of retrieving the wrong papers depends on both. And prefer questions that identify their paper:
on \fresh{}, where 84\% do, retrieval adds 0.8 points instead of 0.3.

\textbf{Limitations.} Fifty questions give wide intervals, and subgroup analyses (29 and 21 questions)
wider still. Mistral-7B was sampled once per question at temperature~0.7, and quantised. Every grade
comes from an LLM judge; controls, inter-labeller agreement, the symmetric re-grade and the second judge
bound but do not remove that dependence, the two judges differ in level, and no human ratings were
collected. \fresh{}'s questions were written by a model, which may make them easier to retrieve than
human-written ones, and a 2025 paper may have had an earlier preprint. The audit labels are themselves
model judgements, and the answer audit proved generous. Our open-world pools depend on public services,
one of which refused most searches; BM25 uses pool-level statistics.

\textbf{Future work.} Human ratings of a sample of the answers on which the two judges disagree would
settle which grading of the wrong-paper answers is right; multiple reference answers per \dq{} question
would remove the need for it.

%% ===================================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Re-evaluated with current models and an automatic, controlled protocol, \dq{} no longer distinguishes
how systems use retrieved context: given the source abstract every model is near perfect, and without
any context strong models already score about 1.4--1.5 of 2, from knowledge of the field rather than
memory of the papers. What separates systems is whether retrieval finds the source, which in an open
world succeeds for 58\% of \dq{} questions and 89\% of our fresh, paper-specific ones; the share of the
oracle's gain that retrieval recovers follows that rate. What retrieving the wrong papers appears to cost
depends on the grading: against a single reference it can cancel the gain, while neither of two judges
from different families finds any cost once correct alternative answers are credited. For scholarly RAG
evaluation, we recommend reporting closed-book and oracle bounds, open-world source recall,
multi-reference grading and a second judge.

\appendix[Prompts]
\label{app:prompts}

\textbf{Closed-book.} \emph{System:} ``You answer questions about computer-science research. Answer in
one to three sentences.'' \emph{User:} the question.

\textbf{Oracle.} \emph{System:} ``You answer questions about computer-science research using the
abstract you are given. Answer in one to three sentences.'' \emph{User:} ``Abstract: \ldots{} Question:
\ldots''

\textbf{RAG and gated.} As the oracle, with ``the paper abstracts you are given'' and the numbered,
titled abstracts.

\textbf{RAG permissive.} ``You answer questions about computer-science research. You are given
abstracts of papers a search returned for the question; they may or may not be relevant. Use them where
they address the question; if none does, ignore them and answer from your own knowledge. Answer in one
to three sentences.''

\textbf{Gate.} ``You check search results for a question about computer-science research. You are given
the question and numbered paper abstracts. Say which abstracts contain information that answers the
question; an abstract on the same broad topic that does not address what is asked does not count.'' It
replies with the numbers of the abstracts to keep.

\textbf{Question writer (\fresh{}).} ``You write questions for a benchmark about computer-science
research, the way its authors did. You are given one paper's abstract. Write one question that the
abstract answers, about the paper's main idea, method, finding or motivation, and its answer: one to
three sentences taken from the abstract. The question must make sense on its own.'' Four \dq{} pairs
follow as examples.

\textbf{Judge.} The benchmark's 0--2 scale with the original paper's worked example (its Table~2); it
grades only against the reference, does not penalise extra correct detail, and does not reward fluency.

\textbf{Multi-reference judge.} The same scale, with the reference and the five retrieved abstracts (the
answer to grade precedes the abstracts): 2 if the answer matches the reference \emph{or} is an equally
valid answer to the question as worded, supported by one of the abstracts or established knowledge.
